That's a clearer angle than the plan's. "Fresh bread is for today, our newsletter is for the whole week" is a contrast a customer gets right away, and it doesn't take a long explanation. Here's my pushback before it goes into your sign-up form:

1. Keep "slow baking" as your internal word, not the customer's word. It works on Instagram with food fans. A busy parent of three won't say it to a friend over coffee, and the friend test ("what does Sam sell?") won't pass with it. In the email, I'd lead with what it gives them, something like "fresh news from the oven, every month", and use "slow baking" as the proof underneath. The wording is yours to find; I'll react to drafts.

2. Expect the objection: "Big stores send emails too." Both have weekly offers and loyalty apps. A customer who has looked into it will raise that within 30 seconds. Your answer is good: store offers aren't stories that keep growing, and a chain can't be tailored to one street's taste. But that answer has to be ready in the content, maybe even as one of the first issues ("Why a coupon isn't a bakery"). If we leave it out, the comparison falls apart the first time someone pushes back.

3. Willow Cafe is your best proof of "made for the week." The order report shows almost 1 in 2 Willow regulars buying your rolls, 6 shifts covered, and 6 standing orders. That's exactly what "made for the week" looks like in practice. With consent granted, the case study (issue #12) can be one of the first issues instead of the last.
